Connect Key Foundation

Co-Founder · Executive Director (2026–Present) · Co-President (2025) · STEM Division President (2023–2024, 2026–Present)

I still test the lessons myself. One of our shyest kids once tugged on my shirt to ask what we were doing next week, and another one yelled “bye, science guy!” on his way out the door. I think about those two a lot more than I think about the hour count.

CKF is much bigger than my part of it. While I am packing kits, our Health Division is running blood pressure checks at a county health fair, the Art Division is tying handmade bracelets onto the wrists of kids in cancer treatment, and the English Division is teaching parents the language their children already speak. Most of it happens in rooms I am never in, run by students I recruited and then got out of the way of.

In 2023 a few friends and I were on a call that went well past 1am, mostly complaining. A lot of the volunteering we’d done was showing up to jobs nobody needed us for. By the end of the call we’d decided to start our own group and let students design the programs. That became Connect Key Foundation. We had already been volunteering together before there was a name for it; CKF was incorporated as a 501(c)(3) in Cerritos in November 2023.

CKF now has five divisions (STEM, Health, Art, English, and Outreach), about 150 student volunteers, and 17+ partners including the YMCA, Discovery Cube, Miller Children’s Hospital, and the Government of El Salvador. Across all divisions, our volunteers have logged more than 50,000 service hours.

CurioCrate is the STEM Division’s hands-on science program, and it’s where most of my time goes. When I became STEM Division president it was a curriculum on paper, without a regular place or schedule to teach it.

I built our partnership with the YMCA, which gave CKF its first permanent STEM lab at the Fairfield Family YMCA. In 2026 CurioCrate became an official YMCA STEM vendor, with sessions running through the summer, fall break, and winter at Fairfield and the Wrigley Youth Center in Long Beach. More than 500 students have now come through CurioCrate, at the YMCA, in weekly after-school sessions at Leal Elementary, at a science camp at Burbank Elementary, and at Discovery Cube.

This year CurioCrate kits started going to classrooms in El Salvador through an official partnership with the Salvadoran government. I put together the student team for that proposal, and we wrote it together.

None of that works if I have to be in the room. So most of my job turned into making lessons that survive a first-time instructor: testing each experiment until it works every time, then writing the supply list, safety steps, and instructor notes that go in the box. I recruit and train the student instructors, now more than 50, who teach from our 40+ original curriculum modules.

The first students I trained teach their own sessions now. Some are Whitney classmates, some are younger siblings who had never cared about science before they had to explain it to a seven-year-old.

The money comes from outside. Society for Science gave CurioCrate a $5,000 STEM Action Grant in 2024, and the Vasant and Prabha Rathi Foundation added $1,500 in 2026. For both, I organized the team and we wrote the applications as a group. Across all divisions, CKF has received more than $30,000 in grants and support, and the office of U.S. Congressman Derek Tran has formally recognized our work.

Case Study: The Lesson That Failed on the Way to Class

The problem. A lesson only counts if a volunteer who has never taught it can run it in a room of 25 kids. Most of my testing happens at my house, with me as the first student to get it wrong.

Failures. Choosing materials took more tries than I want to admit. Wrong quantities, wrong timing, a reaction that works once and never again. I demoed one experiment for the whole team over Zoom from my backyard, and it finally worked. It also left our patio furniture covered in the evidence. My mom has opinions about that week.

The day of. The lesson called for a solution mixed ahead of time. The volunteer bringing the materials brought the raw powder instead. Twenty minutes before class, we had the wrong version of the experiment.

The fix. We taught slime instead. The kids were louder and happier than they would have been with the lesson we planned, which was a strange thing to be relieved about.

What I took from it. The experiment was never the fragile part. The prep was. Anything a person has to remember on the drive over will eventually be forgotten, so it has to be in the box or in the instructor notes. That is the standard I build kits to now.

Research

Independent Researcher, Cal Poly Pomona · Mentor: Dr. K. Anderson · 2025–Present

This is the first problem I’ve worked on that nobody has finished yet, including my mentor. Most of what I do is build a model, take it to something real, and find out where it was wrong. I started in the summer of 2025 and I’m still at it.

An MHD thruster moves seawater with electric and magnetic fields instead of a propeller, so it has no moving parts. The catch is that it’s inefficient. My project at Cal Poly Pomona, with Dr. K. Anderson as my mentor, is about finding a better engine geometry for MHD thrusters on small underwater vehicles.

I run CFD simulations on different geometries, use machine learning tools in the optimization, and build 3D models and printed prototypes of the designs.

This is the project I keep going back to. I’m still working on it with Dr. Anderson, and once a week I drive to Citrus College to take his Mechanics of Materials course, because the project kept needing things I hadn’t been taught yet.

Read the abstract

Prototyping Magnetohydrodynamic Drive Submarines: A Cleaner, Quieter, and Longer-lasting Alternative

Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) propulsion accelerates electrically conductive fluids through the Lorentz force generated by orthogonal electric and magnetic fields, offering a silent, mechanically simple alternative to propeller-based underwater propulsion. Although MHD pumps have been extensively studied, experimental investigations of compact MHD thrusters suitable for small-scale underwater vehicles remain limited. In this study, we designed, fabricated, and tested low-voltage seawater MHD thrusters using 3D-printed housings, neodymium magnets, and copper electrodes. Both rectangular and circular thruster geometries were developed and iteratively refined to evaluate the effects of channel geometry, electrode configuration, voltage, and power on propulsion performance.

Experiments were conducted in seawater-salinity solutions, and propulsion speeds were quantified using video-based motion analysis and flow visualization. Measured velocities were compared with theoretical predictions derived from an acceleration model based on Lorentz-force-driven fluid motion. Results show that propulsion speed increases with applied voltage and electrical power, following the square-root scaling predicted by theory.

Among the tested designs, the optimized circular thruster demonstrated superior structural stability, more uniform force distribution, and higher propulsion efficiency than the rectangular configurations and previous attempts. These results confirm the feasibility of compact, low-voltage MHD propulsion for small underwater vehicles and identify key performance-limiting factors. The findings provide practical design insights for advancing quiet, low-maintenance underwater propulsion technologies at a higher efficiency than previous experiments through geometry optimizations.

The entries above are not four projects. They are one thruster, rebuilt between each of them. Judges at a county fair, a state fair, a Korean science fair, a competition in London, and one in Indonesia each got a different version: rectangular channel, then circular; different electrode spacing; a different way of measuring velocity from the video. Mostly what I took home from those rooms was a list of questions I couldn’t answer yet, and the next build was the answer to one of them.

The work has now left the science-fair circuit. The paper is written with Natalia Bars and Dr. Anderson at Cal Poly Pomona, and I’m the first author: I write the drafts, build the figures, and do the literature review. Dr. Anderson sends it back with notes like “add tick marks to the y axis in Figure 8b” and “say why theory over-predicts the power.” The manuscript went to the IEEE Journal of Oceanic Engineering in August 2026. An extended abstract is in for PRTEC 2027, a thermal engineering conference in Honolulu next March, and a second one is being written for the ASME–JSME–KSME fluids engineering meeting in Seoul that July.

What I’m doing right now is the next question the thruster raised: a CFD model that couples the electromagnetics to the electrochemistry, to see how ion transport, gas bubbles on the electrodes, corrosion, and falling conductivity eat the efficiency.

Haegeum

Co-Founder · President (2026), Haegeum Sarang Orchestra

Weill Recital Hall, Carnegie Hall · June 2024

Carnegie Hall is the line people remember. Before it came almost ten years of weekly lessons on an instrument that very few people outside the Korean community still play.

The haegeum is a two-stringed Korean fiddle with no fingerboard. You get pitch by pressing on the strings in midair, so every note depends on your hand. I started in third grade, so this is my tenth year playing. In June 2024 I played it solo at Weill Recital Hall in Carnegie Hall, at the American Protégé winners’ recital.

I co-founded the Haegeum Sarang Orchestra, a student ensemble within the Korean Traditional Music Institute of America, so more people here would hear this music live. We’ve given 50+ performances at city hall ceremonies, consulate events, and cultural festivals, along with music programs for seniors and underserved communities.

I also play at our church, where the ensemble pieces give way to hymns.

Competitions: award winner at the World Competition of Korean Traditional Performing Arts since 2020. At the American Protégé International Music Talent Competition, Third Place in Fall 2025 and Honorable Mention in Fall 2023.

Civil Air Patrol

Cadet Commander, Fullerton Composite Squadron 56 · U.S. Air Force Auxiliary

My first months as commander looked fine on paper, but it felt like I was fighting the squadron. Things got better when I started handing work off. I gave my first sergeant the lesson plans and promotion boards I’d been keeping to myself, and during her first inspection, which was rough, I made myself stay quiet. She runs whole training blocks without me now, and I’m prouder of that than of any ceremony.

I joined Civil Air Patrol in 2021. I’m now Cadet Commander of Fullerton Composite Squadron 56, one of the largest squadrons in California with 120+ cadets. As the top cadet, I’m responsible for the whole cadet corps and I’m the main link between the cadets and the adult senior members who run the squadron.

I rebuilt our squadron’s administrative systems and wrote year-long training plans, and we’re working toward the CAP Quality Unit Award. Along the way I’ve taught a lot of the squadron’s classes myself.

Science Olympiad

Varsity Team Captain, Gretchen Whitney High School

Science Olympiad gives you a table of events — anatomy, astronomy, chemistry, codebusters — and you try them one by one until something stops feeling like homework. Mine was Helicopter. A flight could be ruined by a closed window or a warm projector in the room, and I could actually find the cause and fix it. Growing up, it was the one place where physics felt completely knowable. My research went the same way later: keep trying subjects until one stops feeling like a detour.

I joined Science Olympiad in 8th grade, and it’s been one of the things I’ve put the most into at Whitney. Five seasons later I’m captain of the varsity team. I redesigned our cabinet system so fewer teammates show up to a competition underprepared.

Computer Science Club

President, Gretchen Whitney High School

Teaching someone their first program reminds me of teaching card magic. In both, you start by asking what effect you want and what the simplest way to get there is. The best part is the same too: the moment they figure out why it works and want to go make their own.

As president of Whitney’s CS Club, I teach Python, Java, and data structures to 30+ students from under-resourced backgrounds. I also help club members get ready for hackathons and USACO, and set up their GitHub portfolios.

Outside school, I bring hands-on coding lessons to partner elementary schools and local nonprofits. Those sessions run in partnership with CKF, which supplies the kits and the volunteer instructors, so a club of thirty can reach a classroom of a hundred.

Youth & Government

Vice President, YMCA Youth & Government · 2024–Present

In committee you learn fast that writing a good rule and getting a rule passed are two different jobs. I spent a year on the second one.

Youth & Government is the YMCA’s model legislature: more than 1,300 students from about 60 delegations across California, two statewide conferences a year, and bills debated in the real chambers in Sacramento. I’m vice president of the Greater Long Beach delegation.

I draft bills, sit on committees, and organize the service projects our delegation runs between sessions. I ran as a Youth Governor candidate and served as Chief Whip on the senate floor, which is the job of counting where the votes actually are before anyone stands up to speak. The two bills I wrote were on prison reform through firefighter camp programs and on expanding civic education in public schools.

At the end of the 2025 session the delegation asked me to give the Year in Review at the Delegate Recognition Banquet. The room was delegates, their families, YMCA staff, and that night’s guest speakers, including the mayor of Long Beach and a California assemblymember. I stood at the podium and talked through the year our delegation had had. Then they named me Outstanding Delegate, and the award came as a Certificate of Recognition from the City of Long Beach.

The Y put a photo of me at the podium on the cover of its 2026–27 registration packet.

Church Youth Group

Youth Group President, New Church · 2023–Present

The slides are the thing. Zone out for two seconds and the congregation loses its place in the responsive reading. Click to the next verse a beat late and the singing goes sideways. I used to sit through service half asleep, doing nothing. Now I can’t, and I didn’t expect to like that.

Our church is small, so everybody does more than one thing. My mom cooks the meal and the snacks after service. My sister does outreach and sings on the praise team. Whoever can play ends up on keyboard or whatever instrument is short that week.

I’ve been there since 2018. My part is the stuff with cables: slides, mics, sound, video. I also write the bulletin and keep the schedule of who is doing what. Since 2023 I’ve led the middle and high school group, which mostly means Bible study on top of all that.

We also raise money for families in the neighborhood who need it.

We serve across our church’s Long Beach, Carson, and Los Angeles services. Our group also joined several other small churches for a mission trip to Mexico, where we built houses, cleaned up neighborhoods, visited families at home, ran programs for the kids, and helped at a medical clinic.

Sometimes I play haegeum for the offering or special music, usually with the pastor’s wife on keyboard. A Korean fiddle on an American hymn, for a congregation that knows both.

Free Flight

Team USA, 2026 FAI F1 Junior World Championships · National Youth Aviation Academy

After one bad practice I was melting down, and my coach said, “There’s no such thing as your day. Focus up and look at the plane.” At Junior Worlds the wind was some of the worst I’ve flown in. Planes broke apart in the air and a few crashed into tents. We still caught thermals on 20 of 23 launches.

F1A is a free flight glider class. You tow the glider up on a line, release it, and after that nobody controls it. Your score is how long it stays up, so most of the work is trimming the plane and reading the air for thermals before you launch.

I was selected for the United States national team and flew F1A at the 2026 FAI Junior World Championships in Prilep, North Macedonia. The United States took first in the F1 Challenge, the championship’s team event, ahead of China and Estonia. In the FAI’s 2025 World Cup standings I was the third-ranked American junior in F1A.

In 2025 I placed 1st at the North America World Championship, the Arizona FF Championships, and the Kotuku Cup, and 2nd at MaxMen International.
